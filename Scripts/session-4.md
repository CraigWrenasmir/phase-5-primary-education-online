### Slide 2

The diagram brings together family, school staff and paraprofessionals, with the student at the centre. Every partner has different knowledge and a different relationship with the student.

What is the purpose of those intersecting partnerships? Take a moment to consider that. It might help to think about whose experience should improve through the decisions made.

No single person holds the complete picture. A student can tell or show us something about their preferences and experience. Families may see things that don't appear at school. School staff and other people involved may notice different contexts or hold useful knowledge about support.

We want that knowledge to come together around the student's access, participation and wellbeing. Ask who is missing from the conversation, and how the student's communication is included. A meeting about a child is not automatically a meeting that has heard them. The key ideas on page eighteen keep this shared purpose in view.

### Slide 3

Power is present in meeting structures, language and decision pathways. It can be shaped by socio-economic circumstances, cultural and historical experiences of education, language, and familiarity with school systems and curriculum.

A school team may be very comfortable with its acronyms, meeting format and procedures. A family may have useful knowledge but little access to those routines. Intentions matter, but so does the experience of the person trying to contribute.

Ask quietly: who sets the agenda? Whose knowledge is heard? Who can make decisions? Is the student included in a way that works for them? What could the school change about the process?

Online and phone meetings have these questions too. Who can join easily? Is a camera expected? Who does most of the talking? Do people have information beforehand and a way to respond afterwards? We can examine those conditions without labelling a family as difficult or disengaged.

### Slide 5

The point to carry forward is the importance of asking students in a way that works for them, and acting on what they communicate. Even young children can express preferences when choices and support are accessible.

What helps? What's hard? What would you like teachers to know? The question, the setting and the way to respond all matter. Where possible, record the student's words or communication accurately, so their view remains part of the record rather than being replaced by an adult summary. That can support the development of self-advocacy over time.

### Slide 6

Consultation literacy means being able to ask, listen and follow up in a useful way. The checklist on page twenty begins with a clear shared purpose and open questions. It includes more than one way to contribute, respect for student and family knowledge, and an accurate record of what was heard.

It ends with an agreed action, responsibility and follow-up. That final part helps distinguish a conversation that felt positive from one that leads to something people understand and can review.

Use the checklist before a meeting and again afterwards. Did people have a real opportunity to influence the discussion? Does the record reflect their contribution? Is it clear what happens next? A tool should support listening, not replace it.

### Slide 7

Let's look at students and families in turn. With students, offer communication choices. That might involve AAC, drawing, writing, symbols, a video or a supported conversation. Prepare them with information about what will be discussed. Offer manageable choices and enough processing time. Think about the sensory conditions of the space.

A trusted supporter can assist communication while keeping the student's voice central. Notice preferences expressed through actions as well as words, and check your interpretation. Build questions around strengths, interests and what matters to the student.

With families, begin with the student's strengths and what works well. Listen to the family's knowledge without assuming that their experience matches the school's. Explain educational language and use real, respectful examples. Families have different cultural experiences, and some parents are neurodivergent themselves.

Check for shared understanding. Agree on next steps and keep communication open. Choose one practice from each column that you would like to strengthen. You can note it privately and carry it into your planning.

### Slide 8

This slide touches on distress and times when capacity runs out. Please look after yourself as you listen, and step away if you need to.

The terms here are meltdown, shutdown and School Can't. We're discussing them without treating them as wilful misbehaviour or a failure of parenting. The experiences and demands leading up to a difficult point may not all be visible at school. Home and school can observe different parts of the same student's day.

Our focus is safety, what preceded the situation, what supports recovery, and what could change in the environment, task or response. What information is missing? What does the student communicate? What can home and school learn from each other?

Keep the discussion within respectful educational practice. There is no need to put a personal crisis story into a group chat. A concern about an individual student belongs with the appropriate support people and school processes. Today we are considering how partnership helps us listen and respond without blame.

### Slide 10

Belonging, capacity and joy help us judge the direction of our work. Belonging means being recognised, included and able to participate as oneself. Capacity brings us back to what the day costs, what protects resources and what restores them. Joy includes interests, connection and authentic expression.

These ideas connect the lens from Session One, the school-day map and the classroom design. They also give partnership a purpose beyond agreeing on a procedure.

Consider your proposed change. Does it reduce a barrier while also protecting something the student values? Is there room for their interests or their preferred way to connect? Quiet compliance or academic performance alone cannot tell us whether a student is flourishing. We need their experience in the picture.

### Slide 12

Let's prepare the next conversation on page twenty-two. Use the bridge notes you made earlier. Choose a real conversation you could have within the next two weeks, with a student's family or with the student themselves.

Write the purpose in one sentence. What do you need to understand? Then fill in what you'll share, beginning with a strength; what you'll ask, as specifically as you can; and what you'll offer. Include how the student's communication and preferences will be heard.

Now write your opening sentence, word for word. It should sound like an invitation to understand together. Leave room for the other person to change the direction of the plan.

Add one possible shared next step and how you would follow up. Note when, where and how the conversation could happen. If you're working with a colleague, you can pause and read the opening sentence aloud, asking whether it sounds open. You can also read it quietly to yourself. Pause for the time you need to plan, then return for a final check.
