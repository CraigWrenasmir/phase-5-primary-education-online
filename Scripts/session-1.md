### Slide 8

A neurodiversity-affirming approach begins with the person. People know, do, learn and connect in different ways. Differences are not automatically deficits, and there is no single correct way for a mind to work.

Everyone has strengths and experiences challenges. So we need room in our thinking for interests, joy and connection as well as difficulty. Being strengths based does not mean overlooking distress or substantial support needs. It means that those needs are understood within a whole person's life.

Consider the students you have in mind. What becomes visible when you include what they enjoy and what they do well? And what disappears if the only thing we describe is behaviour we would like to change?

We can say, this part of the routine asks a great deal of the student, and investigate the support they need. That opens more possibilities than deciding the student is simply unwilling. Our job is to stay curious about access and support.

### Slide 9

The social model helps us look for barriers in places, tasks, routines and expectations. Before asking a student to change, ask what could change around them.

Think about waiting in a noisy lunch line. There might be several demands at once: voices close by, an uncertain wait, people brushing past, and spoken instructions competing with the noise. Those are features we can investigate. We might ask whether there is a clearer sequence, a different place to wait, or a choice that would make access more workable.

Those ideas are possibilities to explore with the student, rather than solutions we can assume. Their communication and experience help us judge whether a change helps. Changing the environment will not make every difficulty disappear. It gives us an important part of the picture that we can act on and review.

### Slide 10

We also need a picture of support that can change. Dynamic disability means that access and support needs can vary with the task, the environment, relationships and available capacity.

A student may manage a routine on one day and need much more support with it on another. The routine may look the same to us, while the demands around it or the resources available to the student are different. What they managed yesterday is useful information; it is not a reason to dismiss what they need today.

Strengths and substantial support needs can both be present. Instead of treating ability as a fixed amount, we can ask: what is being asked here, what support is available, and what is the student communicating now? This is a reason to keep noticing across settings and days.

### Slide 11

Many of us learnt five senses at school: touch, vision, taste, hearing and smell. This slide adds three that are useful for understanding the school day.

Proprioception is our sense of where our body is in space. It includes information from muscles and joints. Think about knowing how much pressure to use when holding a pencil or where your feet are as you move around furniture.

The vestibular sense relates to balance and movement. It helps us make sense of movement and the position of the head. Experiences of stairs, climbing, swinging or sitting still can differ from person to person.

Interoception concerns internal body signals. We'll come back to that shortly, because noticing hunger, thirst or a need for the bathroom is part of daily access too.

These senses work together. A classroom is not just something a student sees. It also has sound, movement, textures, smells and bodily demands. We need to find out how a particular student experiences those things, including what they enjoy.

### Slide 12

Sensory experience includes pleasure and connection, as well as discomfort or overload. Notice sensory joy. That might be an experience a student seeks out or returns to because it feels good or helps them regulate.

Stimming can be part of regulation, pleasure and authentic expression. We should not discourage harmless stimming simply because it looks different. Nor should we treat difference as disruption by default.

Bring one of your students to mind. Where do you see enjoyment, ease or a way of expressing themselves that matters to them? Keep that alongside anything you find difficult to understand. The most useful question may be: what would this student like us to know about this experience?

### Slide 13

The next image builds a whole picture from its details. Detail-first processing means taking in details, then building understanding from those pieces. It is sometimes called bottom-up processing. This is a useful lens on experience, not a test that tells us how every Autistic student thinks.

The guide invites us to notice strengths such as pattern recognition, systems thinking, deep memory and logical precision. It also asks us to recognise honesty, directness, depth of connection and intense engagement. Those strengths matter now; they aren't a consolation prize for something else a student finds hard.

Turn to Strengths I can see on page five. For each student, note one strength you've actually observed and where it appears. Perhaps they notice an inconsistency, remember something important, or understand a topic in unusual depth. Use your own evidence. Pause if you'd like more time.

### Slide 14

Monotropism is a framework for understanding attention going deeply into fewer things at a time. The torch and floodlight on the slide offer a way to picture different distributions of attention. Neither is a better way to be.

A deep interest can provide engagement, learning, memory and joy. When a student is absorbed in a construction task, a drawing or a topic they love, their attention may be strongly invested there. Asking them to stop and pack up for recess can involve much more than putting down a pencil.

The idea of tendrils offers another image: attention has reached into the activity, and shifting means drawing those connections back. A warning, a clear next step or a way to return to unfinished work may help, depending on the student.

We can protect moments of flow while making transitions more accessible. The question becomes how to support a shift, rather than how to make an interest disappear.

### Slide 15

Executive functioning gives us language for the demands involved in doing things. Planning concerns the steps ahead. Organisation helps us manage materials and information. Time management includes noticing time and working within it. Working memory helps us hold information while using it.

Meta-cognition means thinking about our own thinking. Emotional regulation, flexibility and attention affect how we respond and shift. Task initiation is getting started, and persistence is continuing with a task.

These terms are lenses for inquiry, not a checklist for labelling a student. Take an ordinary classroom instruction: finish this, put it away, collect something else and join the next activity. How much must a student remember, sequence, organise and switch? Useful support responds to the particular demand. We'll return to concrete support options in Session Three.

### Slide 16

Let's return to interoception, the sense of internal body signals. Those signals help us make sense of temperature, hunger, thirst and needing the bathroom. They can be part of understanding how we're doing during the day.

Alexithymia means difficulty identifying and describing emotions and feelings. A person might find it hard to work out whether a feeling is excitement or anxiety, or to put a feeling into words. Difficulty naming a feeling does not mean there is no feeling.

The questions on the slide remind us that internal experience may not be obvious from outside. Crying does not tell us the whole story, and an apparently quiet student may still need support. We can offer ways to communicate and remain curious, without claiming to know someone's internal state from one outward sign.

### Slide 17

Now let's apply this to one familiar classroom routine. Open the Sensory activity on page five. You might choose lining up, mat time, visiting the library or eating lunch. Choose something you know well, without naming a student or school.

First, name the routine. Then list what the environment or task asks of students before listing any student responses. Consider the demands you actually notice in your setting. Next, add possible options that could increase access or choice, including a way for students to communicate what helps.

In the final column, write a question to explore. If you think a different place to sit might help, what would you need to ask or observe before assuming that?

You can work privately. If colleagues are watching together, you may pause for a short conversation and compare options. One grounded question is enough. Pause the video for about five minutes, or as long as you need, and then return for the debrief.

### Slide 17

Look back at the order of your notes. Did you begin with environmental and task demands? If you started with a student's response, see whether you can move one step earlier and describe what the routine asks.

Now check the option you've suggested. Does it offer access or choice? How could a student indicate that it helps, that it doesn't, or that they would prefer something else? Different students may need different options within the same routine.

We haven't simulated what it is like to be Autistic. We've examined a routine that educators can change, and identified what we still need to understand. That's the inquiry we'll continue between sessions.
