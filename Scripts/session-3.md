### Slide 2

Students come first. We are moving from understanding to design without turning students into a list of problems. A strategy becomes useful when it answers a particular access question.

Separate an observation from an interpretation. The instruction was given while materials were being handed out describes something we can examine. The student didn't care is an interpretation that closes down inquiry. A question such as, how could the student return to the instruction, points us towards something we can change.

We'll design for one routine, then check the design against each of the students you have in mind. What you don't yet know stays a question for consultation. You don't need a complete explanation of a student before you can begin removing a barrier.

### Slide 3

Keep a concise picture of each student beside the design. What are their strengths, interests and sources of joy? What do you know about their communication, sensory experience and thinking? What costs them effort, and what lightens the day?

Include identity and belonging. Include knowledge already held by the student, family and team. Leave room for open questions.

### Slide 4

Open My brief on page fourteen. Choose one routine you know well. It could be a rotation, a transition or a whole-class routine, and it may be the Reduce item you brought from Session Two.

Complete the three sentence starters. This routine asks students to. For some students, that means. We could change.

For example, a routine might ask students to remember several spoken steps while gathering materials. The access question could be how they find the next step when they need it. That's an example to show the shape of the brief, not an assumption about your students.

Keep your wording specific and within educator or school influence. Remove blame or deficit labels. Where you aren't sure what a demand means for a student, write that as a question.

Pause for around five minutes to draft your brief. You can work privately, or share only your general We could change line with colleagues if that is useful.

### Slide 5

This menu offers four possible directions. Executive functioning supports can answer a known task demand. Predictability gives clear information about routine and change. Schedules make sequence, timing and changes visible. Visual supports can help students find, understand, remember or communicate information.

Listen with your brief in front of you. You are not being asked to add every option. Ask which one, or which pair, might answer the demand you've described. There is space under Options I chose on page fourteen to note the fit.

We'll consider schedules alongside predictability, because keeping a schedule accurate when plans change is part of what makes it useful.

### Slide 6

Executive functioning support should match the demand. If getting started is difficult, making the first step visible may be more useful than repeating the whole task. If the task asks students to hold several instructions in mind, a sequence they can return to may reduce that demand.

Organisation might involve making materials easier to locate or showing what is needed before a task starts. Time can be made clearer with information about what will happen and when. A transition may be easier to approach with preparation and a way to finish or return to the current work.

These are examples to consider, rather than a checklist to apply to every student. Look at your brief. What exactly are you helping the student to do? How will they be able to tell you, in a way that works for them, whether the support helps?

A support should reduce unnecessary demand. If it adds more adult prompting or pressure without improving access, we need to look again.

### Slide 7

Predictability means having useful information about routine and change. It doesn't require every day to be identical, and it isn't a promise that nothing unexpected will happen.

Think about how much information in a primary classroom is held by adults, spoken once, or implied. Can students find out what is happening, what is changing, what comes next, and what they can do if the plan changes? Give that information early enough to be useful.

A schedule is one way to make sequence and change visible. The example on the slide is an assembly moving earlier. The class schedule needs to change too. Move the card and explain the change, rather than leave students to discover that the displayed plan is wrong.

Then ask whether students can return to that information and communicate a question or need for support. A schedule that is accurate, understood and available offers access. A schedule that students cannot interpret or that no longer reflects the day may add confusion.

### Slide 8

A visual support should clarify something the student needs to find, understand, remember or communicate. Begin with that need, rather than with a tool you would like to use.

Objects, photographs, symbols and written words are possibilities. A visual is not automatically accessible. Does the student understand what it represents? Can they reach or find it at the point of use? Does it reflect what is actually happening, including change? Does it give useful information or a way to respond?

Those quick-review questions are on page fifteen. If your design uses a visual support, run through them. Explain how the support works and check its meaning with the student. Avoid unnecessary detail.

Remember the distinction between understanding information and expressing a message. A schedule may support understanding, while a student's AAC gives ways to communicate. Keep both forms of access in view.

### Slide 9

Universal Design for Learning gives us another way to examine the emerging design. Is important information clear and available? Are there workable ways to participate and respond? Does the design support engagement while keeping meaningful challenge?

For example, a learning goal can remain worthwhile while students have more than one way to show what they know. The information students need can be available in a form they can return to, rather than depend on remembering one spoken explanation.

Individual knowledge and universal design can inform each other. What we learn from a particular student's experience may reveal a barrier affecting others too. Making an option available to everyone can improve access, while some students will still need something more specific. The presence of a resource alone does not tell us access has improved.

### Slide 10

Let's check your draft. On page fourteen, choose one or two options that seem to answer your brief and note why they fit. Then turn to the Accessibility check on page fifteen.

Can students find and understand what they need? Can they take part in more than one way? Can they pause, ask for help or recover? Is the support available when demand increases?

If an answer is not yet, adjust the design. Perhaps information is available at the start but disappears when the task begins. Perhaps a student can see what to do but has no workable way to ask a question.

You don't need a perfect design on paper. Make the next version more useful, and keep the questions you will test through experience. Pause for a few minutes to work through the check, then come back.

### Slide 11

Page sixteen is My design. We're going to pull the change together in five parts.

First, state the barrier or unnecessary demand. Second, describe the change to the environment, task or routine. Third, explain how students' communication and experience will be included. Fourth, decide what you will observe. Fifth, set a time to review.

Make the measure about access. Can a student find the information they need, communicate a preference or participate in a way that works for them? Finishing faster or appearing quieter does not, on its own, tell us the experience improved.

Keep the change small enough to try within your role. If you are watching with a colleague, you can pause and explain your design in a couple of minutes, then invite one question about the barrier, the change or the review. On your own, read it back using those same questions.

The original activity allows around seven minutes of design time and a pair check. Pause now for the amount of time you want, then resume for the debrief.

### Slide 11

Look at the connection between your first and second steps. Does the change actually address the barrier you named? If not, revise either the understanding of the barrier or the proposed support.

Now check the review. Who will help you find out whether access has improved? What will the student's communication contribute? An adult observation is one part of the evidence, not the whole account.

Keep a one-sentence description of your change and the access question it addresses. The design remains open to refinement; you are not committing to keep a support that turns out to be unhelpful.

Does one student need information in another form? Might another need a different way to respond or more individual support? Keep the focus on what the design offers and what still needs to be understood.

### Slide 12

A different result for each student is useful information. It reminds us why we've held several students in mind across the series. Where the design helps, it may be doing valuable work. Where it makes no difference or someone needs more, that is a reason to consider targeted support or an individual adjustment. Universal design and individual support can work together.

### Slide 13

The continuum of support helps us keep that whole picture in view. An inclusive environment might have clear routines and a classroom culture where different ways of participating are accepted and valued.

Universal supports could include instructions available in different forms, flexible ways to show learning or movement breaks that any student can access. Targeted support responds to a more particular demand. A small group might benefit from pre-teaching vocabulary or additional support to organise independent work.

Individual adjustments respond to a particular student's access and participation needs. The examples in the guide include a quieter assessment workspace, additional processing time, a visual task breakdown or another way to demonstrate learning.

These layers are connected. We can ask what is available to everyone, what is targeted, and what is individually adjusted. None of that should be decided from diagnosis alone. Consultation, the day map and evidence from using the support help us understand what is needed. Review remains part of each response.

### Slide 14

Reasonable adjustments are changes or supports that help students access and participate in education. They should respond to the student and the barriers they experience, and be practical and effective in that context.

The slide places environmental design and Universal Design for Learning alongside the NCCD levels of adjustment. Those levels are quality differentiated teaching practice, supplementary adjustments, substantial adjustments and extensive adjustments.

Keep the distinction clear: adjustments are the actions and supports. The NCCD is the process for collecting information about adjustments and the evidence for them. It does not replace the work of listening, trying a support and reviewing what happens.

For decisions about an individual student, follow the processes in your school or system and work with the relevant team. Here, we're connecting the design work to those processes, rather than making a determination about any student's level.

### Slide 15

The Nationally Consistent Collection of Data on School Students with Disability, or NCCD, connects adjustments with evidence. Use current official NCCD information and the consultation, documentation and review processes used by your school or system.

The line on this slide states that under the Disability Standards for Education 2005, schools must consult the student, or an associate such as a parent or carer, about adjustments. Keep the student's communication and experience central to that consultation.

Make responsibilities clear. Who will ask, who will put the adjustment in place, what evidence will be gathered, and when will the team review it? Take local procedural questions to the appropriate school or system contact. Page twenty-four of the Participant Guide points to official NCCD information.
