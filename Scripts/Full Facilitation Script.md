### Slide 8

A neurodiversity-affirming approach begins with the person. People know, do, learn and connect in different ways. Differences are not automatically deficits, and there is no single correct way for a mind to work.

Everyone has strengths and experiences challenges. So we need room in our thinking for interests, joy and connection as well as difficulty. Being strengths based does not mean overlooking distress or substantial support needs. It means that those needs are understood within a whole person's life.

Consider the students you have in mind. What becomes visible when you include what they enjoy and what they do well? And what disappears if the only thing we describe is behaviour we would like to change?

We can say, this part of the routine asks a great deal of the student, and investigate the support they need. That opens more possibilities than deciding the student is simply unwilling. Our job is to stay curious about access and support.

### Slide 9

The social model helps us look for barriers in places, tasks, routines and expectations. Before asking a student to change, ask what could change around them.

Think about waiting in a noisy lunch line. There might be several demands at once: voices close by, an uncertain wait, people brushing past, and spoken instructions competing with the noise. Those are features we can investigate. We might ask whether there is a clearer sequence, a different place to wait, or a choice that would make access more workable.

Those ideas are possibilities to explore with the student, rather than solutions we can assume. Their communication and experience help us judge whether a change helps. Changing the environment will not make every difficulty disappear. It gives us an important part of the picture that we can act on and review.

Source and adaptation: Primary Education Online Session 1, slide 9; supplied slide and speaker notes; Facilitator Guide p. 16. Participant Guide p. 3. Authored online delivery adaptation: optional private reflection, no asserted audience replies.

### Slide 10

We also need a picture of support that can change. Dynamic disability means that access and support needs can vary with the task, the environment, relationships and available capacity.

A student may manage a routine on one day and need much more support with it on another. The routine may look the same to us, while the demands around it or the resources available to the student are different. What they managed yesterday is useful information; it is not a reason to dismiss what they need today.

Strengths and substantial support needs can both be present. Instead of treating ability as a fixed amount, we can ask: what is being asked here, what support is available, and what is the student communicating now? This is a reason to keep noticing across settings and days.

Source and adaptation: Primary Education Online Session 1, slide 10; supplied slide and speaker notes; Facilitator Guide p. 17. Participant Guide p. 3. Authored online delivery adaptation: optional private reflection, no asserted audience replies.

### Slide 11

Many of us learnt five senses at school: touch, vision, taste, hearing and smell. This slide adds three that are useful for understanding the school day.

Proprioception is our sense of where our body is in space. It includes information from muscles and joints. Think about knowing how much pressure to use when holding a pencil or where your feet are as you move around furniture.

The vestibular sense relates to balance and movement. It helps us make sense of movement and the position of the head. Experiences of stairs, climbing, swinging or sitting still can differ from person to person.

Interoception concerns internal body signals. We'll come back to that shortly, because noticing hunger, thirst or a need for the bathroom is part of daily access too.

These senses work together. A classroom is not just something a student sees. It also has sound, movement, textures, smells and bodily demands. We need to find out how a particular student experiences those things, including what they enjoy.

Source and adaptation: Primary Education Online Session 1, slide 11; supplied slide and speaker notes; Facilitator Guide p. 18. Participant Guide p. 3. Authored online delivery adaptation: optional private reflection, no asserted audience replies.

### Slide 12

Sensory experience includes pleasure and connection, as well as discomfort or overload. Notice sensory joy. That might be an experience a student seeks out or returns to because it feels good or helps them regulate.

Stimming can be part of regulation, pleasure and authentic expression. We should not discourage harmless stimming simply because it looks different. Nor should we treat difference as disruption by default.

Bring one of your students to mind. Where do you see enjoyment, ease or a way of expressing themselves that matters to them? Keep that alongside anything you find difficult to understand. The most useful question may be: what would this student like us to know about this experience?

### Slide 14

Monotropism is a framework for understanding attention going deeply into fewer things at a time. The torch and floodlight on the slide offer a way to picture different distributions of attention. Neither is a better way to be.

A deep interest can provide engagement, learning, memory and joy. When a student is absorbed in a construction task, a drawing or a topic they love, their attention may be strongly invested there. Asking them to stop and pack up for recess can involve much more than putting down a pencil.

The idea of tendrils offers another image: attention has reached into the activity, and shifting means drawing those connections back. A warning, a clear next step or a way to return to unfinished work may help, depending on the student.

We can protect moments of flow while making transitions more accessible. The question becomes how to support a shift, rather than how to make an interest disappear.

Source and adaptation: Primary Education Online Session 1, slide 14; supplied slide and speaker notes; Facilitator Guide p. 21. Participant Guide p. 3. Authored online delivery adaptation: optional private reflection, no asserted audience replies.

### Slide 15

Executive functioning gives us language for the demands involved in doing things. Planning concerns the steps ahead. Organisation helps us manage materials and information. Time management includes noticing time and working within it. Working memory helps us hold information while using it.

Meta-cognition means thinking about our own thinking. Emotional regulation, flexibility and attention affect how we respond and shift. Task initiation is getting started, and persistence is continuing with a task.

These terms are lenses for inquiry, not a checklist for labelling a student. Take an ordinary classroom instruction: finish this, put it away, collect something else and join the next activity. How much must a student remember, sequence, organise and switch? Useful support responds to the particular demand. We'll return to concrete support options in Session Three.

Source and adaptation: Primary Education Online Session 1, slide 15; supplied slide and speaker notes; Facilitator Guide p. 22. Participant Guide p. 3. Authored online delivery adaptation: optional private reflection, no asserted audience replies.

### Slide 16

Let's return to interoception, the sense of internal body signals. Those signals help us make sense of temperature, hunger, thirst and needing the bathroom. They can be part of understanding how we're doing during the day.

Alexithymia means difficulty identifying and describing emotions and feelings. A person might find it hard to work out whether a feeling is excitement or anxiety, or to put a feeling into words. Difficulty naming a feeling does not mean there is no feeling.

The questions on the slide remind us that internal experience may not be obvious from outside. Crying does not tell us the whole story, and an apparently quiet student may still need support. We can offer ways to communicate and remain curious, without claiming to know someone's internal state from one outward sign.

### Slide 2

Even in an online session, people may be communicating through words, reactions, facial expression, or a choice about when to speak. Communication takes many forms, and speech is one part of it.

At school, a request to use your words can accidentally close off a way a student is already communicating. Pointing, signs, writing or a communication device are valid ways to share a message too. We can notice the message and make sure there is a workable way for it to be received.

Source and adaptation: Primary Education Online Session 2, slide 2; supplied slide and speaker notes; Facilitator Guide p. 29. Participant Guide p. 7. Authored online delivery adaptation: optional private reflection, no asserted audience replies.

### Slide 3

Understanding and expressing are connected, but they aren't the same thing. Receptive communication concerns information coming in and how a person makes sense of it. That might include spoken words, writing, visual information, demonstration or gesture.

Expressive communication is how a person sends a message out. It may involve speech, writing, typing, movement, sign or AAC. AAC stands for augmentative and alternative communication. It can include a communication board or book, signs, typing or a device. Some people use it alongside speech and some use it instead of speech.

How much someone speaks does not tell us how much they understand. Fluent speech doesn't guarantee that all spoken instructions were understood. Limited speech doesn't mean limited understanding. Communication can also change with the person, the setting and the demands of the moment.

Think about one task in your classroom. How can a student receive the information, and how can they respond? Those are two access questions, and both deserve attention.

### Slide 5

There are three useful starting points on this slide. Clear communication means using familiar words and offering one idea or step at a time. It also means being specific enough that the person doesn't have to guess what we intend.

Processing time means giving time to understand, organise a response and communicate it. A silence can feel long online, or in a busy classroom. Filling it with a second question or a new version of the instruction may add more information to process. We can practise letting the pause be there.

Visual supports mean showing information as well as saying it. A photograph, written instruction, object or sequence may help, where the student understands and can access it.

These are options to match to the person and moment. Keep their preferred ways to respond available, including a way to refuse, ask for help, or say they need more time.

Source and adaptation: Primary Education Online Session 2, slide 5; supplied slide and speaker notes; Facilitator Guide p. 32. Participant Guide p. 7. Authored online delivery adaptation: optional private reflection, no asserted audience replies.

### Slide 6

Double empathy asks us to look at understanding in both directions. When people have different experiences and ways of communicating, understanding one another can be harder for both people.

If an interaction goes wrong, we should not automatically locate the whole problem within the Autistic student. The expectations of adults and peers also shape what happens. Whose expectations are operating during group work, a classroom discussion or a conversation in the playground? Are those expectations clear, and do they allow more than one way to connect?

A student's preferred style might be direct, brief, detailed, quiet, or based around a shared interest. We can ask what makes the exchange clearer and safer for everyone. Connection does not need to look one particular way to be meaningful.

Source and adaptation: Primary Education Online Session 2, slide 6; supplied slide and speaker notes; Facilitator Guide p. 33. Participant Guide p. 7. Authored online delivery adaptation: optional private reflection, no asserted audience replies.

### Slide 7

The picture of connection reaches beyond the classroom. Students may connect with family, at school, in their community or online. Culture and individual experience shape what connection means and how it is expressed.

Some students enjoy talking one to one. Others enjoy making something alongside another person, sharing a deep interest, playing, or simply being together quietly. Frequent conversation is only one possible form of connection.

We don't need private details about a student's online life or community membership to keep our thinking broad. Let's bring this back to the kinds of connection you actually notice at school, and to what the students themselves communicate about them.

### Slide 9

The battery image gives us a way to talk about energy and capacity. A day does not always begin with the same charge. Activities can use resources, and what happens across the day can affect what is available later.

Some people use the language of spoons: having a limited number available, using some for each action, and finding things harder when spoons are low. These are metaphors that help us ask about experience, not a way to measure a student's internal state.

The picture also includes recharging. Support, recovery, familiar connection or something enjoyable may restore some capacity. Something can be worthwhile and still take effort. Keep both the cost and what lightens it in mind as we map the day.

It ends with an agreed action, responsibility and follow-up. That final part helps distinguish a conversation that felt positive from one that leads to something people understand and can review.

Use the checklist before a meeting and again afterwards. Did people have a real opportunity to influence the discussion? Does the record reflect their contribution? Is it clear what happens next? A tool should support listening, not replace it.

### Slide 7

Let's look at students and families in turn. With students, offer communication choices. That might involve AAC, drawing, writing, symbols, a video or a supported conversation. Prepare them with information about what will be discussed. Offer manageable choices and enough processing time. Think about the sensory conditions of the space.

A trusted supporter can assist communication while keeping the student's voice central. Notice preferences expressed through actions as well as words, and check your interpretation. Build questions around strengths, interests and what matters to the student.

With families, begin with the student's strengths and what works well. Listen to the family's knowledge without assuming that their experience matches the school's.
